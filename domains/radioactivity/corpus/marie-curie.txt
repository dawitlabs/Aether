Marie Curie was born Maria Skłodowska in Warsaw in 1867. In 1891 she moved to Paris to study physics and mathematics, and in 1895 she married the physicist Pierre Curie. She coined the word "radioactivity" while studying the rays first reported by Henri Becquerel.

Working with Pierre, she discovered two new elements in 1898. She named the first one polonium, after her native Poland, and the second one radium. In 1903 she shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel, becoming the first woman to receive a Nobel Prize.

After Pierre died in 1906, she took over his teaching post and became the first woman to hold a professorship at the University of Paris. In 1911 she received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery of radium and polonium, which made her the first person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences. She led the physics and chemistry laboratory of the Radium Institute in Paris.

During the First World War she organised mobile X-ray units that brought radiography to field hospitals. She died in 1934 of aplastic anaemia, most likely caused by years of exposure to radiation. The element curium is named in honour of Marie and Pierre Curie.
