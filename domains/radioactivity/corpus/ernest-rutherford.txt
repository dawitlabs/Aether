Ernest Rutherford was a physicist born in New Zealand in 1871. In 1899 he showed that the rays given off by uranium were of at least two kinds, which he named alpha and beta radiation. Working with Frederick Soddy around 1902, he explained radioactivity as the transformation of one element into another, and he introduced the idea of the half-life. He received the 1908 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this research on the disintegration of the elements.

Experiments carried out by Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden in his laboratory, in which alpha particles were fired at thin gold foil, led Rutherford in 1911 to propose that an atom's positive charge and most of its mass sit in a tiny central nucleus. Later he showed that alpha particles could knock protons out of nitrogen nuclei, turning nitrogen into oxygen: the first nuclear reaction produced on purpose.

In 1919 Rutherford became director of the Cavendish Laboratory at the University of Cambridge. Under his leadership, James Chadwick discovered the neutron there in 1932. Rutherford died in 1937.
